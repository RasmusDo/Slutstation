# Selling tickets yourselves — the legal position, honestly

You asked me to make sure the terms cover everything so nobody can sue you and
you can't get in trouble with the law. I can't give you that, and I'd rather say
so plainly than write something that sounds reassuring. What terms can do is
make you *right* when a dispute happens, so it ends quickly and cheaply and in
your favour. What they cannot do is take away rights Swedish law gives the
buyer — a clause that tries is simply struck out, and the rest of your terms are
then read with suspicion.

Everything below is researched from Swedish primary sources — statutes,
Konsumentverket, and published ARN decisions. It is not legal advice and I am
not a lawyer. Two things in here are worth paying a Swedish advokat a few hours
for; I've marked them.

---

## The clause you asked me to write is void

You wanted: refunds under extreme circumstances, but no refund when the
cancellation is force majeure or otherwise outside your control. Your current
terms already say exactly that: *"We are not responsible for delays, changes, or
cancellations caused by factors beyond our reasonable control… Tickets will not
be refunded in such events."*

That clause does not work in Sweden. **ARN 2020-04504** dealt with wording so
close to yours it could be the same paragraph — an organiser whose terms said
that where the event cannot go ahead due to an obstacle outside the organiser's
control, the organiser is free from any obligation to refund. ARN struck it down
under **36 § avtalslagen** and ordered the full refund. The reasoning matters
more than the outcome: the terms were imposed unilaterally, they shifted the
entire risk to the consumer with nothing given in return, and — this is the line
that should change how you think about it — **the organiser had chosen not to
insure, and instead passed that risk to ticket buyers.**

Konsumentverket says the same thing without hedging: if a concert is cancelled
or moved you get your money back, *"oavsett anledningen till att arrangören
ställer in eller ändrar datum, även om de hänvisar till så kallat force
majeure."*

The principle underneath is older and simpler than consumer law. Someone who
does not receive the performance they paid for does not have to pay for it. No
event, no entitlement to the ticket money. Force majeure is a defence against
*damages* — it stops a buyer claiming their train fare, their hotel, the evening
they took off work. It is not a defence against giving back the price of a thing
you didn't deliver.

So I've written the terms the other way round, which is the position you now
hold: **cancelled or materially changed means the full ticket price back,
whatever the cause** — and we expressly exclude the surrounding costs, which is
the part force majeure genuinely does protect. That's not a concession. It is
the only version that survives contact with ARN, and it reads far better to a
buyer than a clause that would have failed anyway.

One nuance worth knowing, because it's the only crack of daylight: in **ARN
2020-04925** a cycling event whose terms *capped* refunds at 50% in
extraordinary circumstances was upheld as reasonable. A pre-disclosed,
proportionate, clearly-signposted partial retention can survive. A blanket "we
keep it all" cannot. You chose the clean version, which I think is right for a
scene brand where goodwill is worth more than 25 kr an order.

Two related points, both now in your terms. You may **offer** a ticket to a
future event instead of cash, but you may never impose one — ARN 2020-05928
rejected exactly that, noting the specific date is usually the whole reason
somebody bought. And if you postpone, the buyer chooses: new date, or money
back. A one-year postponement in ARN 2020-04664 was held to be something the
parties plainly hadn't contemplated, and the full 20 000 kr went back.

## The good news you didn't ask about

**There is no 14-day right of withdrawal on your tickets.** Distansavtalslagen
2 kap 11 § p. 12 excludes cultural and similar leisure activities supplied on a
specific date. This is a genuine and significant advantage of your category —
you never have to take a ticket back just because somebody changed their mind.

Two conditions on it, both now handled. You must still *tell* buyers the right
doesn't exist; silence is a breach even though the substance is in your favour.
That's why there is now an explicit tick at checkout saying so. And the
exclusion depends on the ticket being for a **specific date** — an undated pass,
a season ticket or a gift card is probably outside it and probably *does* carry
14 days. If you ever sell one of those, come back to this.

## The thing that actually worries me, and it isn't the terms

Your site says access is strictly for members, and the assumption underneath —
that a members-only event is a private event — is wrong in a way that carries a
criminal penalty.

**Ordningslagen 2 kap 3 §** anticipates exactly this move. An event whose
admission is limited by invitation, **by membership of a particular
association**, or by any other condition *is nonetheless treated as an event the
public has access to* where it's obviously part of an operation that mainly
consists of arranging such events, or where — given the size of the circle
admitted and the conditions of admission — it is comparable to a public event.

Selling tickets openly online to anyone who signs up for a free membership fails
that test on every limb. The circle is unbounded, the condition is trivial and
self-service, and arranging these events is what the association does.
**RH 1997:40** is close to your facts and went against the organisers: 500
invitation cards handed out broadly, names written in by the recipients, 30 kr
at the door — held to be a public event, not a closed circle. The contrast case,
**RH 2000:12**, is an acquittal, and shows how far away it is: 80–100 people,
numbered personal tickets given free to known individuals, a pre-approved guest
list checked at the door, no advertising, no sales at the door.

**Ordningslagen 2 kap 29 § carries fines or up to six months' imprisonment**,
and it attaches to the *anordnare* — named people, in practice board members,
not an abstract legal person. Alongside that: the police can order the event
stopped and bill you for policing, and a missing permit will typically void your
event insurance at the moment you most need it.

There's a second reason the membership wrapper doesn't help you. To be an
allmännyttig ideell förening for tax purposes you must satisfy the
**öppenhetskrav** in inkomstskattelagen 7 kap 10 § — anyone who shares the
association's aims and follows the stadgar must be able to join. You cannot tell
Skatteverket you're open to all and tell the police you're a closed circle. The
tax answer is the one you need.

**None of this means you have to stop.** The fix is cheap and mostly
administrative — the dance-permit requirement was abolished on 1 July 2023, so
for an event at your own or a rented venue you now only need to *notify* the
police, which is free.

### What to actually do

1. **Treat every ticketed event as an offentlig tillställning.** Stop reasoning
   from the membership.
2. **On public land** — a street, a park, a square — you need a **tillstånd**
   from Polismyndigheten (2 kap 4 § first paragraph). Apply in writing, normally
   at least **one week** ahead; in practice apply far earlier.
3. **At your own or a rented venue** — an **anmälan** to the police, at least
   **five days** ahead. Free.
4. **Write to the local tillståndsenhet describing exactly what you do** — open
   online ticket sales, free membership on signup, outdoor location released to
   ticket holders — and ask them to confirm in writing what they require. A
   written answer costs nothing and is the single best defence you can hold.
5. **Fix the association's stadgar and the site copy so they agree.** Membership
   can be a condition of buying a ticket; it cannot be described as making the
   event private.

*Worth an advokat's hour.* Not because the answer is unclear — I think it's
fairly clear and against you — but because the downside is personal criminal
liability for named board members, and that's not a risk to carry on my say-so.

## Alcohol

If you serve alcohol you need a **serveringstillstånd** from the *kommun*, not
the police (alkohollagen 8 kap 1 §). There's no non-profit exemption.

The same closed-circle trap applies. A "slutet sällskap" permit requires the
members to be known in advance and sharing a genuine common interest; **if
unknown people can buy or take out membership at the entrance, it is not a
slutet sällskap.** Apply for a **tillfälligt serveringstillstånd till
allmänheten**, per event. Lead times of four to eight weeks are normal, so this
has to go in the calendar at the same moment you book a venue.

Two things that catch club applications specifically: for permits to serve the
public, 8 kap 15 § requires a suitable food offering and kitchen, which is often
the binding constraint; and serving without the right permit is criminal under
alkohollagen 11 kap and will poison every future application and your
ordningslagen position with it.

## Tax, and a threshold that selling direct makes closer

As an allmännyttig ideell förening you're very likely outside VAT entirely —
mervärdesskattelagen 4 kap 6 § says an activity isn't "ekonomisk verksamhet"
where the income is income you're not liable to income tax on. Entry fees to
your own cultural events are the textbook case of *naturlig anknytning* and are
income-tax exempt, so no output VAT on tickets (and no input VAT deduction on
sound, lights or artist fees either).

There's also a backstop: the **omsättningsgräns is 120 000 kr** per calendar
year, raised from 80 000 kr on 1 January 2025, and it's automatic.

Here's the part that changes because you're selling direct. Through a platform
you may only ever see the net remittance. Selling yourselves, the **gross**
ticket revenue is yours — and it's the gross figure that counts against that
threshold and feeds the 75% test behind your allmännyttig status. It is entirely
possible to be comfortably under on platform-net and over on direct-gross. If
you cross mid-year, VAT bites **from the transaction that crossed it**, not from
next year, and you can't retroactively add it to tickets you already sold.
**Watch gross ticket plus bar plus merch monthly, not annually.**

One piece of good timing: VAT on entry to *danstillställningar* dropped from 25%
to 6% on **1 July 2026**, so the old and slightly absurd question of whether
your audience is facing the stage or dancing no longer changes the rate.

*Worth an accountant's hour* if you expect to go past 120 000 kr this year.

## What I changed on the site

The **Event terms & Conditions** accordion is gone, replaced by two: **Köpvillkor
för biljetter / Ticket purchase terms**, and **Ordningsregler / Event terms &
conduct**. Swedish first, English translation after, and the Swedish text
governs — which is what the law expects when you sell to Swedish consumers, and
what ARN will read.

The purchase terms now carry the things Swedish distance-selling law makes
mandatory and your old terms didn't have: who the seller is and the
organisationsnummer (e-handelslagen 8 § — **there's a `[ORGNR]` placeholder in
two places you need to fill in**); that the displayed price is the total
including VAT and every fee with no booking fee added at checkout
(prisinformationslagen 10 §); how the purchase works and when it becomes
binding; that there is no ångerrätt and why; the refund position above; what a
refund doesn't cover; refused entry; that Stripe handles payment and you never
see a card number; and **ARN by name, with its postal address, and a statement
that you follow its recommendations.**

There is **no ODR link**, and if you have one anywhere else, remove it. The EU
Online Dispute Resolution platform **shut down on 20 July 2025** (Regulation (EU)
2024/3228 repealed the ODR Regulation). A dead ODR link is now itself misleading
information under marknadsföringslagen.

One duty most small traders miss, and it's cheap to get right: **lag 2015:671
11 § requires that when you refuse a consumer's claim, you tell them about ARN
at that moment, in durable form.** Put the ARN sentence in your standard refusal
email template — not just on the website.

At checkout, the pay button now says **"Betala 250 kr"**, not "Checkout".
Distansavtalslagen 2 kap 9 § says a consumer is bound by an order carrying a
payment obligation *only if* that obligation was made clear beforehand and
expressly accepted — and the sanction isn't a fine, it's that the buyer was
never bound and can walk away with their money. A button reading "Continue" or
"Slutför" is the classic way to fail this. There's also a required tick
confirming the terms and the absence of ångerrätt, and Stripe's own page now
shows both facts above its pay button.

On photography I changed the legal basis. "By entering you consent" is weak —
consent under GDPR has to be freely given, and walking through a door isn't
that. The terms now rely on **legitimate interest** with a clearly stated right
to object, on the spot or afterwards, and close-ups where an individual is the
subject only with that person's consent. That is both more defensible and more
decent.

Finally, every order now records **which version of the terms it was sold
under**. Bokföringslagen 7 kap 2 § makes the documents that establish the terms
of a transaction part of the accounting record you keep for seven years, and in
a dispute the question is never what your terms say today — it's what they said
the day that person bought. Bump `terms_version` in app_settings whenever you
change the Köpvillkor.

## Why doesn't everyone do this? Your instinct was right

You said it seemed fishy that it's this easy and nobody else does it. Two
answers, and both are worth reading.

**First, the premise is only half true.** Small and mid-size promoters self-sell
all the time — club nights, 200-cap rooms, tickets under 200 kr, on sale a few
weeks out. That works fine. But almost nobody at scale runs their own acquiring.
fabric moved *to* Resident Advisor as its sole outlet. Way Out West states
plainly that everything goes through Ticketmaster. Berghain's answer to
ticketing is not to sell in advance at all. What looks like a venue selling
direct is usually **white-label**: the checkout sits on the venue's domain and
carries its branding, but the ticketing company remains the merchant of record.
That distinction is the entire product.

**Second — and this is the catch — the checkout was never the hard part.**
Ticketing is a deferred-delivery business wearing e-commerce clothes, and every
hard thing about it is on the payments side.

Stripe won't stop you. Event ticketing appears nowhere on its restricted
businesses list. You'll onboard in ten minutes and nobody will ask a question.
The risk is applied later, by behaviour: Stripe's reserves policy names
"longer-than-average delivery windows" and an "unexplainable sharp increase in
processing volume" as triggers, and an on-sale is *literally both of those at
once* — a quiet account suddenly doing 900 000 kr in ninety minutes for goods
deliverable in eight months. From a risk model's point of view that is
indistinguishable from a pre-order scam. The industry classes ticketing as
high-risk under MCC 7922 and prices it accordingly.

The number that should stay with you is the **chargeback window**. For Visa
reason code 13.1, services not received, the clock does not start at the
transaction. It runs **120 days from the date the buyer expected to receive the
service**, capped at **540 days from the transaction**. Sell a ticket in August
for a festival next June and your liability on that one payment runs about
fourteen months. And if you cancel, you cannot win any of those disputes —
there's no evidence to file, the service wasn't provided. Stripe's dispute fee
in Sweden is **200 kr**, refunded only if you win, so a lost dispute on a 250 kr
ticket costs you 450 kr — nearly twice the ticket.

Then the part nobody expects: **you don't save money.** On the Nordic model —
Billetto, Resident Advisor, DICE, Tickster — the fee is paid by the *buyer*. Your
gross is 250 kr either way. Moving to your own Stripe checkout doesn't save you
5 kr; it *costs* you about 5.55 kr per ticket in processing. What you actually
gain is a ticket that's 20–30 kr cheaper for your audience, your own customer
data, control of the experience, and escape from Billetto's 33% commission on
tickets their advertising brought you. Those are real and, for a scene brand,
probably worth it. But they are the gains, not "we keep the booking fee".

And what you take on in exchange is the thing the fee was buying: **merchant of
record status**. Eventbrite states it outright — when you use their payment
processing, *Eventbrite* is the merchant and manages chargebacks on your behalf.
Every platform also pays out *after* the event: Eventbrite holds a 20% reserve
and settles three days after; Billetto pays three to five working days after;
Skiddle says it plainly. That cash-flow annoyance is not a flaw in their
product. It is the product. It physically prevents you from spending the
customer's refund money before you've earned it.

The history is not hypothetical. When Festicket collapsed, **Stripe was holding
about £7.7m**, and in June 2024 a judge ruled the promoter contracts created no
trust over it; £5.39m was still locked at Stripe. Tickets.ie ceased trading in
June 2026 owing about €1.4m to 34 organisers. Eventbrite lost $146.5m in Q1 2020
and needed a $225m credit facility, essentially because it had paid organisers
before the events happened. And Sweden gives ticket buyers **no protection at
all** if you go under — resegarantilagen covers package travel, not standalone
tickets. A buyer's claim against you is worth exactly what you're worth.

So: build it, sell through it, and **do the one thing the platforms do that
costs you nothing to copy — don't spend ticket money before the doors open.**
Nothing in Swedish law requires you to ring-fence it. That is precisely why it's
on you. The admin panel now shows "held for events not yet held" as its own
figure so the number is in front of you every time you log in. Treat it as
somebody else's money, because legally that's very close to what it is.

Two more things while you're small enough to design them in. **Buy cancellation
insurance** — ARN specifically held against an organiser who chose not to insure
and pushed the risk onto buyers, so this is both risk management and a better
legal position. And **never sell tickets on behalf of another promoter out of
your own Stripe balance**: taking money owed by buyers to a third party is
potentially a regulated payment service needing Finansinspektionen authorisation.
If you ever host a guest promoter, use Stripe Connect so Stripe holds the funds.

## The short list

1. Fill in `[ORGNR]` in the Köpvillkor — two places in `index.html`.
2. Write to the police tillståndsenhet; file an anmälan for every event (or a
   tillstånd on public land). Keep the reply.
3. Apply for a tillfälligt serveringstillstånd till allmänheten, per event, four
   to eight weeks ahead, if you serve alcohol.
4. Put the ARN sentence into your standard refund-refusal email.
5. Get cancellation insurance before the first on-sale.
6. Don't spend money shown under "held for events not yet held".
7. Watch gross revenue against the 120 000 kr VAT threshold monthly.
8. Have an advokat read the Köpvillkor and give you a view on the ordningslagen
   position. That's a couple of hours, and it's the only part of this where I'd
   spend money.
